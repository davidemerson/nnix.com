Extremely compact. It's a bit over a hundred pages without padding, no anecdote stretched to fill a chapter, no idea restated three times for the benefit of an imagined skimmer. Hollis makes his point and moves on. I wish more books in this territory were built this way, and the parsimony is a good part of why this one is useful.

It's Jungian, which I expected to be an impediment to relevance, but it was not, this is a fully modern interpretation of Jung, and welcome.

Not every reader is going to relate to every element of the middle passage Hollis illuminates, a lot of which involves independence from parents. Despite that, what's here is practical and broadly applicable.

Core idea: midelife is the collapse of a provisional identity. In the first part of your life, he asserts that you assemble a self out of what family and culture demanded, layered over reflexive strategies formed around early experience

 (his recurring framing is the child's sense of being either overwhelmed or abandoned, and the lifelong compensations that follow).
 
 That constructed personality works until it doesn't. The Middle Passage begins when it stops: when career, marriage, children, and achievement no longer carry the meaning that was projected onto them, and the distance between the acquired personality and the actual Self becomes unignorable.
 
 Most midlife marital distress, he argues, is the withdrawal of projection: we marry a figure unconsciously expected to complete us, heal any parental wound, and live our unlived life on our behalf. When the projection dissolves, the partner stands revealed as a separate person with their own history, and the couple either interprets that as the failure of the marriage or treats it as the start of an actual relationship.
 
 In second adulthood,
 - personal authority replaces inherited authority, 
 - meaning replaces satisfaction,
 - one increases tolerance for ambiguity,
 - one relishes genuine solitude,
 - mortality is accepted as the thing that gives remaining time its weight.
